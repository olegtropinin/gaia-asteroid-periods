\title{Candidate Rotation Periods for 34 Main-belt Asteroids from Gaia DR3 Sparse Photometry}

\author{Oleg Tropynin}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, Kazakhstan}

\begin{abstract}
I searched Gaia DR3 sparse photometry for asteroid rotation periods with a periodogram that combines
season offsets, a linear phase term and a second-order Fourier series. Among the numbered asteroids
that Gaia observed most often, I analysed the 300 highest-ranked with a known period (validation set)
and the 298 highest-ranked without one (target set). On 337 confident results that can be compared with
published periods, 80\% agree within 3\%. Accuracy is 81--89\% for periods below 50\,h and 36\% above it,
where the method usually returns half of the true period. I report 34 asteroids that had no rotation
period in LCDB, JPL SBDB, DAMIT, SsODNet, about 180 VizieR tables, or the Minor Planet Bulletin 2023--2026, as
targets for confirmation. An independent reanalysis of all of Gaia DR3 with a refined method recovers the
same period for all 34.
\end{abstract}

\section{Data and method}

I ranked the numbered asteroids by the number of Gaia DR3 Solar-System-object observations
\citep{Tanga2023}. From the top of this list I took the first 300 that have a rotation period of
2--100\,h in JPL SBDB, which follows the October 2023 revision of the LCDB \citep{Warner2009}, as the
validation set. I took the first 298 without a JPL period as the target set. Every selected asteroid has
at least 443 Gaia observations. Gaia photometry was averaged per transit, with a 0.03\,mag error floor.
For every transit, JPL Horizons gave the heliocentric distance $r$, the distance $\Delta$ from Gaia and
the phase angle $\alpha$, with Gaia as the observer. Times were corrected for light time.

The reduced magnitude $G-5\log_{10}(r\Delta)$ was fit by weighted least squares with one offset per
observing season, a linear phase term and a second-order Fourier series in rotational phase. I scanned
2--100\,h on a frequency grid with a step of $0.1/T$, where $T$ is the time span. Because most asteroid
lightcurves are double-peaked, I adopted the doubled period whenever doubling raised $\chi^2$ by less than
a factor of 1.5. This factor was chosen by two-fold cross-validation on the validation set. The confidence
score combines the $\chi^2$ gain over a no-rotation model with the separation of the best minimum from the
next one outside the $\times1$, $\times2$ and $\times\frac12$ aliases. The confidence threshold was set on
the validation set, at the score where precision reaches 80\%.

\section{Validation}

The cross-validated recovery rate on the validation set is 67.7\%. Above the threshold, I compared every
result whose period is known (Figure~\ref{fig:1}):
\begin{itemize}
\item the validation set: 172 of 214 correct. This figure is in-sample, because the same set fixed the
threshold, and is therefore optimistic;
\item target-set asteroids that \citet{Durech2023} solved from the same Gaia data with a different method:
81 of 97 agree. This tests the method, not the data;
\item 25 of my initial candidates that later appeared in \citet{Cellino2024}, \citet{MacLennan2026} or
\citet{McNeill2023}: 16 of 25 agree.
\end{itemize}
In total, 268 of 336 (80\%) agree.

There are two failure modes. Above 50\,h the method usually returns half of the true period (10 of 28
correct). Slow rotators with periods beyond 100\,h, such as 214, 818 and about 4{,}600\,h, receive
spurious short periods.

\section{Results}

The 34 candidates are all main-belt asteroids. In October 2026 they were absent from:
\begin{itemize}
\item JPL SBDB and LCDB~V4.0;
\item \citet{Durech2023};
\item DAMIT \citep{Durech2010};
\item the SsODNet compilation \citep{Berthier2023}, in a snapshot of its ssoBFT table;
\item about 180 VizieR tables (5.1 million rows) that hold asteroid rotation periods;
\item the full text of the Minor Planet Bulletin, volumes 50--53.
\end{itemize}

Of the 34 candidates, 30 have periods below 50\,h, where I expect about 83\% to be correct. Four have
periods above 50\,h.

An independent reanalysis of all numbered asteroids with at least 25 Gaia DR3 transits used a refined method
\citep{Tropynin2026b}. It recovers
the same period, within 3\%, for all 34. It also led me to drop seven earlier candidates:
\begin{itemize}
\item four with periods above 50\,h, where the true period is most likely twice the value I found
(7699, 14372, 43014, 104169);
\item three where a slow solution is now preferred (13686, 39932, 109412).
\end{itemize}
The SsODNet check removed one more candidate: \citet{Durech2022} give 19.7181\,h for 11857 from ATLAS
photometry, which agrees with my 19.710\,h.

Periods, alias periods, amplitudes, amounts of data and flags are
available at \url{https://github.com/olegtropinin/gaia-asteroid-periods} and archived on Zenodo
\citep{Tropynin2026}, together with the code and the validation tables. I offer the list as targets for
dense-lightcurve confirmation.
