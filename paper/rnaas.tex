\title{Candidate Rotation Periods for 34 Main-belt Asteroids from Gaia DR3 Sparse Photometry}

\author{Oleg Tropynin}
\affiliation{Independent researcher, Kazakhstan}

\begin{abstract}
I searched Gaia DR3 sparse photometry of 598 well-observed numbered asteroids for rotation periods,
fitting at each trial frequency a linear model with season offsets, a linear phase-angle term and a
two-harmonic Fourier series. On 336 confident results that can be compared with published periods,
80\% agree within 3\%; accuracy falls to 36\% above 50\,h, where the method usually returns half of the
true period. I list 34 asteroids whose confident period was absent from all major period compilations,
as targets for confirmation. An independent reanalysis of Gaia DR3 with a refined method recovers the
same period for all 34.
\end{abstract}

\section{Data}

I ranked the numbered asteroids in the Gaia DR3 \texttt{sso\_source} table \citep{Tanga2023} by their
number of observations and kept the 6{,}000 best observed. Going down this list, I took the first 300
that have a rotation period of 2--100\,h in JPL SBDB, which follows the October 2023 LCDB
\citep{Warner2009} (\emph{validation set}), and the first 298 that have no JPL period (\emph{target set}).

Gaia reports one $G$ magnitude per CCD crossing. For each transit (\texttt{transit\_id}) I averaged the
epochs and the magnitudes of its CCD observations. The uncertainty $\sigma_i$ of transit $i$ is the
largest relative flux error among its CCDs, converted to magnitudes and added in quadrature to a
0.03\,mag floor. JPL Horizons, with Gaia as the observer, gave for each transit the heliocentric distance
$r_i$, the distance $\Delta_i$ from Gaia and the phase angle $\alpha_i$. Epochs were corrected for light
time, $t_i \to t_i-\Delta_i/c$. A new \emph{season} starts wherever consecutive transits are more than
60\,d apart.

\section{Method}

For a trial frequency $f$ the reduced magnitude is modelled as
\begin{equation}
G_i-5\log_{10}(r_i\Delta_i)=c_{s(i)}+\beta\,\alpha_i+\sum_{k=1}^{2}\left[a_k\cos(2\pi k f t_i)+b_k\sin(2\pi k f t_i)\right],
\end{equation}
where $s(i)$ is the season of transit $i$. All coefficients are free and enter linearly: one magnitude
offset $c_s$ per season, which absorbs the change of viewing geometry and aspect between seasons, a
common phase-angle slope $\beta$, and four Fourier amplitudes $a_1,b_1,a_2,b_2$. They are solved by
weighted least squares with weights $\sigma_i^{-2}$, giving $\chi^2(f)$. Before the search, transits
deviating by more than five robust standard deviations ($1.4826\times$ median absolute deviation) from
the fit without Fourier terms are removed, in two passes.

I evaluated $\chi^2(f)$ on a uniform grid from 0.24 to 12 rotations per day ($P=1/f$ from 100 to 2\,h)
with a step of $0.1/T$, where $T$ is the time span of the data. The best frequency $f_1$ gives the peak
period $P_1=1/f_1$. Because most lightcurves are double-peaked and the second harmonic can fit them at
half the rotation period, I adopt $2P_1$ whenever $\chi^2(f_1/2)<1.5\,\chi^2(f_1)$ and $2P_1\le100$\,h.
The factor 1.5 was chosen by two-fold cross-validation on the validation set. The amplitude is the
peak-to-peak range of the fitted Fourier series.

The confidence score is $S=\ln(\chi^2_0/\chi^2_1)\,(\chi^2_2/\chi^2_1-1)$, where $\chi^2_0$ is the fit
without Fourier terms, $\chi^2_1=\chi^2(f_1)$, and $\chi^2_2$ is the lowest $\chi^2$ farther than $5/T$
from $f_1$, $2f_1$ and $f_1/2$. The first factor measures how much rotation improves the fit, the second
how much the best peak stands out. The threshold $S\ge0.1604$ is the lowest score at which the
highest-scoring validation results reach 80\% agreement within 3\%. The code is archived with the data
\citep{Tropynin2026}.

\section{Validation}

The cross-validated recovery rate on the full validation set is 67.7\%. Above the threshold:
\begin{itemize}
\item validation set: 172 of 214 agree (in-sample, because this set fixed the threshold);
\item target-set asteroids that \citet{Durech2023} solved from the same Gaia data with a different
method: 81 of 97 agree;
\item target-set asteroids published since by \citet{Cellino2024}, \citet{MacLennan2026} or
\citet{McNeill2023}: 15 of 25 agree.
\end{itemize}
In total 268 of 336 (80\%) agree. Accuracy is 81--89\% below 50\,h and 10 of 28 above it, where half
the true period is usually returned. Slow rotators beyond 100\,h receive spurious short periods.

\section{The 34 candidates}

Of the 298 target-set asteroids, 165 pass the threshold. I removed the 97 solved by
\citet{Durech2023} and every asteroid with a rotation period, in October 2026, in JPL SBDB, LCDB~V4.0,
DAMIT \citep{Durech2010}, the SsODNet ssoBFT table \citep{Berthier2023}, about 180 VizieR tables
(5.1 million rows), or the Minor Planet Bulletin volumes 50--53. Of the 42 that remained, a
reanalysis of all of Gaia DR3 with a refined, independently calibrated method \citep{Tropynin2026b}
dropped seven (four probably at twice the period found here, three now preferring slow solutions),
and \citet{Durech2022} already gives 19.72\,h for 11857 from ATLAS photometry. The remaining 34
(Table~\ref{tab:1}) are all main-belt asteroids with 58--91 transits in 4--7 seasons, and the reanalysis recovers the same period within
3\% for each of them. From the validation, I expect about 83\% of the 30 periods below 50\,h to be
correct. The repository lists for every candidate the alias period, the number of seasons, the time
span and the score. I offer the list as targets for dense-lightcurve confirmation.
